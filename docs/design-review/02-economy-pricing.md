# Adversarial review: economy and pricing

Reviewer: independent economy adversary. Scope: retail pricing, demand, supplies, wages, hiring costs, improvement costs, and the financial feedback loop. Review date: 2026-10-04. Production code was not edited by this reviewer.

The economic structure supports a management game, but its exact constants are hypotheses. No retrieved source establishes that elasticity 1.8, a 60% campaign, or a $20 pack is optimal. The previous $5 retail ceiling is unsupported. The largest remaining risk is a player interpreting production as profit while customer demand and consumable spending are hidden or underexplained.

## Retrieved evidence

1. **Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek, MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research**, retrieved successfully as the original PDF from [Northwestern](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf). Relevant passages: "iterative, qualitative and quantitative analyses" support refinement; "thinking about the player encourages experience-driven (as opposed to feature-driven) design"; the Tuning section explicitly calls for "play testing and tuning." This supports checking financial dynamics against the intended experience and using human observation. It does not prescribe idle-game constants or prove enjoyment.
2. **David Sirlin, Balancing Multiplayer Games, Part 1: Definitions**, retrieved successfully from [Sirlin.net](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-1-definitions). Relevant passage: "Lots of meaningful choices presented to the player. They should be presented with enough context to allows the player to use strategy to make those choices." The article also warns that options that do the same thing or lose to a dominant option add learning complexity without interest. Its domain is competitive multiplayer. Only the contextual choice and unnecessary complexity principles are transferred here. An incremental game can deliberately be an optimization puzzle; opponent-dependent depth is not a requirement for Cube Farm.
3. **David Sirlin, Game Balance and Yomi**, retrieved successfully from [Sirlin.net](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/game-balance-and-yomi). Relevant passage: "Balance itself doesn't make a game fun." Its discussion of preserving flavor, dynamics, and elegance cautions against mechanically flattening the office incentives solely to equalize payback.
4. **Universal Paperclips implementation**, retrieved successfully from the [live main.js](https://www.decisionproblem.com/paperclips/main.js). `raisePrice()` raises the margin by one cent without a gameplay ceiling; `lowerPrice()` has a one-cent floor. Marketing affects demand through `Math.pow(1.1, marketingLvl-1)`. This is a primary example of the requested game's pricing interaction, not evidence that copying its values would balance Cube Farm. Its sales formula differs materially from Cube Farm's deterministic rate.
5. **Anthony Pecorella, The Math of Idle Games, Parts I and II**, direct [Part I](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-i) and [Part II](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-ii) requests returned HTTP 403. The historic Kongregate blog route returned 404; the current Kongregate route returned 403. This reviewer does not treat unavailable text as retrieved evidence. Exponential-cost conclusions below follow directly from Cube Farm's equations.

## Price and hiring sensitivity

For a steady production rate `P`, demand `D = K × p^-e`, no contract, and enough inputs:

- Gross retail income is `min(P, K × p^-e) × p`.
- On the production-limited side, increasing price raises revenue.
- On the demand-limited side, revenue is `K × p^(1-e)`. With `e > 1`, lowering price raises gross revenue until all production sells.
- The clearing price is `(K/P)^(1/e)`.
- At that price, gross income is `K^(1/e) × P^(1-1/e)`. For `e = 1.8`, production raises revenue sublinearly, with exponent 0.444. Wages and supplies grow linearly. More hires can eventually reduce profit unless the market grows.

This is not an unavoidable race to the bottom across the game. Marketing, sales, acquisitions, reputation, and projects grow `K`. It is a real overproduction trap within a fixed market. That trap needs an actionable explanation, not an assertion that all hires are good.

The user's reported office has `P = 17.9`, `D = 15`, and `p = $0.65`. Treating these rounded numbers as a steady snapshot, holding other variables fixed:

| Elasticity | Clearing price before marketing | Gross income/s | Clearing price after one ×1.6 campaign | Gross income/s |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.2 | $0.5610 | $10.04 | $0.8299 | $14.86 |
| 1.5 | $0.5777 | $10.34 | $0.7903 | $14.15 |
| 1.8, current | $0.5892 | $10.55 | $0.7650 | $13.69 |
| 2.2 | $0.5998 | $10.74 | $0.7427 | $13.29 |

Current gross income at the posted price is $9.75/s. Eighteen base-wage employees cost $1.62/s; 17.9 forms/s cost $1.432/s before logistics. The steady break-even price if every produced form sells is about $0.1705, excluding premiums/upkeep. These calculations do not reconstruct the user's exact run, climate, worker activity, or software revision. The posted $8.13 net figure equals gross minus base wages and omits the modeled supply expense, so it is not evidence of a currently reproduced accounting bug.

The first salesperson raises the unmodified market coefficient from 6 to 13.5, a factor of 2.25. The second raises it from 13.5 to 21, a factor of 1.556; the third raises it from 21 to 28.5, a factor of 1.357. Diminishing relative benefit and lost production give sales a contextual tradeoff. In a constrained 18-person office, the first assignment is probably unusually efficient. In a three-person production-limited office it is probably harmful. This should be tested with imperfect policies, not smoothed away merely to equalize headline percentages.

At unchanged production, each ×1.6 campaign raises the clearing price by `1.6^(1/1.8) = 1.2984`, so repriced gross revenue grows approximately 29.84%. Campaign costs rise 70%, which naturally lengthens payback. Its usefulness above the old $5 cap is one reason that cap conflicts with the rest of the economy.

No numerical source establishes these costs. Existing `BALANCE.md` shows coffee payback changes from 274.1 seconds with three workers to 12.7 seconds with 36 workers in deliberately unconstrained sales examples. This supports the intended scale-sensitive return, not universal balance. Equipment's corresponding 117.3 and 102.7 seconds show that an improvement can remain competitive as scale changes. All such comparisons must include supply expense and current demand. Morale recovery is explicitly excluded in the current preview, which should remain disclosed.

Cultivar base hire prices $35/$95/$260/$850 and research costs $0/$80/$300/$1,100 create capital commitments before higher rates become available. Revenue gates $0/$150/$750/$3,500 prevent all recruitment options appearing immediately. These price ratios are not proportionate to output rates 0.65/1.3/2.2/4.8, so better cultivars need capital but not wage premiums unless their genes add them. This supports replacement over infinite headquarters hiring, but it leaves early cultivars mainly as stepping stones. That is acceptable in a changing incremental loop, provided research and sales specialization preserve useful distinctions and existing employees retain their historical traits.

Upgrade base prices $50/$85/$75/$100/$160 and maxima 12/8/6/8/5 are finite authored office content. Equipment adds linear output; training and quality compound remaining risks; memo affects only a temporary boost; capacity unlocks more hiring. Because these purchase types affect different bottlenecks, a universal fixed best-buy list is misleading. The code's marginal-return display is preferable to marketing every level as automatically profitable. The exact maxima and geometric costs remain playtest hypotheses.

## Evidence limits and acceptance conditions

Existing legal formula-informed playthroughs complete in about 58 to 65 minutes. They show solvability and a bounded optimized path. They do not show that an uninformed player will discover sales, preserve procurement reserves, avoid overhiring, understand delayed morale, or enjoy pricing. MDA and Sirlin both caution against inferring the player experience from numerical balance alone.

This reviewer rejects the old $5 gameplay cap and misleading links between active sales output and retail reach. The retained economy is conditionally justified by coherent bottlenecks, forgiving recovery, and contextual investments. Final acceptance requires consistent price validation across action/UI/save boundaries, no profitable hire-transfer loop, explicit demand and procurement recovery paths, and imperfect-player simulations or human observations that address the posted 18-person bottleneck. No source supports a claim of award quality or addiction.


## Final implementation review

The independent follow-up inspected `price.ts`, `CorporationSchema`, the price action/input, `tickAutomation`, `memoRetailReturn`, emergency receipt accounting, and the new regression tests. `bun test src/game/design-review.test.tsx src/game/supply-demand.test.tsx` passed 15 tests with 89 assertions during this review. No production edits or test changes were made by this reviewer.

- **Price decision accepted.** New actions round to cents and reject nonfinite, nonpositive, sub-cent, and unrepresentable values. Valid prices above $5 survive simulation and schema parsing. Historical fractional prices are preserved rather than silently rewritten. The lower bound is the smallest supported USD denomination. The upper bound protects integer-cent calculations and is not described as a market rule. There is no current correctness blocker in this path.
- **Specialist research niche accepted provisionally.** The new 2.5 multiplier gives specialists a meaningful research purpose after executives appear. Tests compare expected uptime-adjusted research and production at QA 0 and 8. This addresses a dominance problem rather than adding a new system. Those two comparisons do not prove that 2.5 is the optimal multiplier, that all combinations are balanced, or that first-time players recognize the niche.
- **Automatic memo retail guard accepted.** `memoRetailReturn` subtracts $20 and additional blank-form spending from additional sales, and accounts for existing inventory over the 90-second window. Purely saturated retail no longer causes repeated unprofitable automatic memos. Procurement disabled with insufficient stock is tested. A manual memo remains an intentional option with an explicit warning.
- **Noncash memo purposes accepted with limits.** Research, influence, compliance while pressure exists, and a still-unfinished order can justify memo use even without positive immediate retail return. This is deliberately broader than cash optimization. The automatic policy does not value insights or influence against a player's next goal, so it can still spend on a department that the player no longer values. The configured standing instruction expresses consent to that behavior, and reserves bound the spending. Do not advertise it as globally optimal automation.
- **Delegated order funding guard accepted.** Renewal requires cash for reserve, the remaining supply budget for half allocation, and estimated wages until escrow pays. It does not borrow hypothetical future retail receipts. A high-output, low-cash national-order case remains idle. This is conservative financial protection, not a promise of success under every random downtime sequence or changing crisis. Manual order acceptance continues allowing player risk.
- **Emergency reserve accepted.** The previous recovery claim was incomplete once non-removable acquired payroll exceeded emergency receipt production. The additive saved reserve now protects actual emergency filing receipts up to one current supply pack, capped by available cash during payroll. Buying supplies clears the reserve, and resumes ordinary accounting. No money is granted by the reserve. The regression uses 84 acquired employees, no headquarters employees, spent relief, zero stock/cash, and 16 actual filings; it verifies $20 survives payroll and buys stock. Old saves default to zero protected receipts. The $20 schema maximum follows the existing pack price, not a separately invented bank subsidy.

A newly supplied actual player save held approximately $1.65 billion, maximum marketing, the former $5 price, and overbuilt Network infrastructure. This changes the confidence statement: the old price ceiling demonstrably compressed the pricing choice in a real late game, while uncapping it can increase company income still further. Plentiful cash cannot solve a replication/discovery allocation halt under the current Network equations. Normal infrastructure costs are not consequential resource choices in that save. Therefore price removal corrects the market rule but must not be claimed as the solution to late-game waiting, cash scarcity, or overall depth. The Network review owns the allocation and pacing response.

The revised economy acceptance is conditional, not a fun certification. Price/recovery/automatic-spending correctness objections above are addressed. Retail sales reach remains headcount/cultivar based, independent of active influence yield; instructions should make that distinction visible. Imperfect informed simulations and the actual player's complaint add evidence, but a first-time human playtest is still necessary to establish whether production, market growth, and payroll are understandable and enjoyable.
