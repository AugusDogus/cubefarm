# Adversarial review: staffing and departments

Reviewed 2026-10-04. Scope: headquarters staffing, department allocation, cultivar niches, wages, morale, pressure, transfers, replacements, branch cohorts, and the interface cost of managing them. This is an independent design critique of the implementation, not a claim that automated completion proves enjoyment.

## Research actually retrieved

1. Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek, [MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research](https://www.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf). Downloaded and extracted the PDF. Relevant quotation: "game components cannot be evaluated in vacuo, aside from their effects on a system behavior and player experience." Its tuning section explicitly calls for "play testing and tuning." This supports evaluating staffing by the player experience it creates, rather than counting roles as depth. It supplies no numerical formula for the appropriate wages or morale target.
2. Ian Schreiber, [Level 7: Decision-Making and Flow Theory](https://gamedesignconcepts.wordpress.com/2009/07/20/level-7-decision-making-and-flow-theory/). Retrieved the complete HTML article. Relevant quotation: "Blind decisions have an effect on the game, and the answer is not obvious, but there is now an additional problem: the players do not have sufficient knowledge on which to make the decision." Also: "Obvious decisions could be automated." These are an experienced designer's teaching framework, not controlled evidence that any particular staffing screen is fun.
3. Ian Schreiber, [Level 3: Transitive Mechanics and Cost Curves](https://gamebalanceconcepts.wordpress.com/2010/07/21/level-3-transitive-mechanics-and-cost-curves/). Retrieved the complete HTML article. Relevant quotation: "In general, if you give players a choice, try to make those choices give about the same benefit; if it is a choice between two equally good things, that choice is a lot more interesting than choosing between an obviously strong and an obviously weak effect." The article explicitly acknowledges guesses and the need for playtesting. This motivates comparing cultivar opportunity costs and policy dominance. It does not require every early unit to remain optimal forever in an incremental progression game.
4. Jakob Nielsen, [Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/). Retrieved complete HTML. Relevant quotation: "You have to disclose everything that users frequently need up front, so that they have to progress to the secondary display only on rare occasions." This supports keeping department counts and bulk hiring visible while putting individual cohort inspection in details. It is general application UX guidance, not game pacing research.
5. Nielsen Norman Group, [User Control and Freedom](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-control-and-freedom/). Retrieved complete HTML. Relevant quotation: "When a user makes a change to the status of a system, he should be able to easily undo that." Reversible assignments and cancelable release confirmations support experimentation. An economic transfer cannot be perfectly undone without restoring historical people and money, so confirmation is justified even though freely reversible role changes need none.

## Verdict and concrete objections

The four roles produce a credible allocation problem: operations fund expansion, research opens tools, sales expands demand and earns influence, and compliance offsets pressure. Individual trait inheritance makes allocation more interesting than four interchangeable counts. Bulk assignment and cohort management are necessary to preserve that problem at scale.

The implementation does not yet make the consequences sufficiently legible. Its bulk assignment objective is also narrower than a player may assume. Three issues warrant a response before claiming this system is finished:

1. **Blind allocation.** `Departments.tsx` previews only the number of remaining operations employees. Moving the best worker to research may sharply reduce sales income, or may have no cash cost when production already exceeds demand. The same assignment could be wise or harmful, but the count screen supplies neither a revenue estimate nor production/demand/research/pressure deltas. Recommended fix: preview the exact draft staffing plan's expected output, demand, insights, pressure relief, and sustainable cash change, labeled estimates. Use the same assignment calculation for preview and application. A general warning to keep some operations staff does not solve this.
2. **Research cultivar niche is weak.** Current `departmentRate` gives specialists double research yield, but an executive has base rate 4.8 versus a specialist's doubled 4.4, with lower downtime and the same recurring base wage. A direct calculation on the real lab fixture with aptitude 1 and no genes produced 1.4135 insights/s for a specialist and 1.5655 for an executive. Higher purchase price preserves a short-term capital choice, but specialists are not actually the best sustained researchers despite the claimed specialty. Candidate tuning: specialist research multiplier 3, making raw research rate 6.6 versus executive 4.8. This is a hypothesis chosen to preserve a niche, not a number established by research. Test equivalent traits at early and fully upgraded states, then check whole-run pacing before retaining it.
3. **Policy choices are asymmetric.** With every incentive installed, zero pressure, no story/path bonuses, and morale at equilibrium, balanced output multiplier is 1.15 at wage multiplier 1; lean is 1.325 at wage multiplier 0.8; humane is 1.08 at wage multiplier 1.2. One strong compliance worker can erase lean's pressure growth, especially after equipment and Legal framework. Humane is then a welfare/expressive choice, not an equally competitive economic choice. Do not claim symmetric optimization strategies. Either identify it explicitly as prioritizing employee welfare at a profit cost, or create a distinct mechanical benefit and verify it has a useful regime. Simply increasing its output to erase all costs would undermine the ethical tradeoff.

## Verification boundaries and suggested acceptance checks

Read the actual domain logic and UI components listed above. Executed a direct Bun calculation against the real lab fixture to compare unmodified specialist/executive research yields. Executed the policy equilibrium arithmetic from current formulas. No production files or tests were changed for this review.

Existing tests substantiate gene role specialization, effective inherited researcher selection, invalid count rejection, atomic operations replacement, protected department employees, and history preservation. They do not substantiate human fun, equal policy appeal, legibility of future staffing cash consequences, or an executive-versus-specialist research niche.

If the proposed changes are adopted, add semantic checks for matched-trait specialist research superiority, staffing forecast agreement with applied allocations, fixed-cultivar demand deltas, and costs including inherited wage modifiers. Compare naive all-research/all-production allocations against recovery behavior. Retain the original full-run pacing ceilings. Do not change a completion threshold merely because a revised niche makes one seed slower.

Human observation remains necessary: ask a first-time player to solve a demand bottleneck and explain whether moving someone to sales sacrifices forms, whether humane policy is chosen for welfare or profit, and whether they understand a branch executive is not a headquarters salesperson. The system can be considered mechanically sound before these sessions, but its decisions cannot honestly be declared fully enjoyable or award-worthy without them.
