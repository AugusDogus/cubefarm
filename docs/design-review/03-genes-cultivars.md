# Inherited traits and employee profiles: adversarial design review

Reviewer: independent `genes_review` agent. Reviewed 2026-10-04. Scope: recruitment, inherited traits, staffing, transfers, replacement, and the biotech fiction. This is a design audit, not proof that players enjoy the system.

## Research actually retrieved

- Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek, [MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf). PDF retrieved and extracted with `pdftotext`. Short quotation: "iterative, qualitative and quantitative analyses support the designer". The paper connects mechanics to emergent dynamics and the resulting experience. It supports auditing traits in the complete economy, rather than deciding that more buttons equal depth. It does not prescribe two slots, five genes, or any current multiplier.
- David Sirlin, [Balancing Multiplayer Games, Part 1: Definitions](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-1-definitions). HTML retrieved and parsed. Short quotation: "Lots of meaningful choices presented to the player." He adds that players need context for strategic decisions. His subject is competitive multiplayer. Applying its viable-options principle to a single-player optimization game is an explicit analogy, not a claim of experimental evidence for idle games.
- David Sirlin, [Balancing Multiplayer Games, Part 2: Viable Options](https://www.sirlin.net/articles/balancing-multiplayer-games-part-2-viable-options). HTML retrieved and parsed. Short quotation: "omit needless words, mechanics, characters, and choices." He argues that differently useful options create valuation problems, while strictly superior options reduce strategy. This supports checking actual role niches and removing fake choices. It does not require every unlocked tier of an incremental game to remain equally good forever.

Access limitations: no human playtest was performed by this reviewer. The sources are a framework paper and expert practitioner writing, not controlled evidence that this game's particular genetics fiction or numbers are enjoyable. No source justifies the exact costs or percentages below.

## Measured objection: the Specialist research niche

At the reviewed 2x specialty multiplier, a `labFixture()` company with otherwise identical unmodified employees and aptitude 1 produced this analytical average (7.5-second work / 4.5-second downtime approximation used by cohort and balance analysis):

| Profile | Base hire cost | Potential insights/s | Average insights/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Generalist | $35 | 0.23363 | 0.19175 |
| Processor | $95 | 0.46725 | 0.37865 |
| Specialist | $260 | 1.58147 | 1.41354 |
| Executive | $850 | 1.72524 | 1.56555 |

Executive was approximately 10.75% better per desk than Specialist in research, while having the same ongoing wage. Specialist had much better return on recruitment capital, but that is a temporary niche once cash is abundant. The comparison is an analytical estimate, not measured human gameplay. The existing specialization test varied gene profiles on a Specialist only, so it could pass while this cultivar problem remained.

Recommendation: retain Generalist/Processor as recruitment steps, keep Executive as a production/sales top tier, and give Specialist an explicit research advantage. If the parent changes the multiplier, add a semantic test comparing Specialist with Executive using equal aptitude, genes, and office conditions across meaningful upgrade levels. Preserve the original gene-role tests. Do not lower test expectations to make a weak niche pass.

## Reviewer conclusion

The genes make sense as speculative corporate fiction and as three role-oriented templates. They do not currently establish a rich combinatorial build game. The strongest defensible parts are inherited history, free future-profile experimentation, production versus research versus compliance suitability, and bulk replacement that avoids rewriting people. The arbitrary parts are exact slot count, costs, unlock gates, and multipliers. The concrete defect is the reviewed Specialist research multiplier failing its apparent role promise. The economically dominated Fire action is acceptable only as explicitly requested flavor.

Remaining validation should examine whether a new player understands future-hire inheritance, whether three role templates feel worth creating, and whether bulk replacement is used without regret. Automated yield tests cannot answer those questions or establish award-quality enjoyment.
