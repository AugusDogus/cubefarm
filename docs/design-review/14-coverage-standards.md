# Adversarial review: coverage and evidence standards

Reviewer: independent decision-coverage agent. Reviewed the production game and component modules, App, styles, DESIGN, BALANCE, README, and the deterministic player. This report audits integration and omissions across the domain reviews. It does not certify human enjoyment or prescribe production changes from authority alone.

## Research actually retrieved

Retrieved on 2026-10-04 using HTTP requests, not search snippets:

1. Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, Robert Zubek, [MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/MDA.pdf), HTTP 200, PDF extracted to text. Its central distinction is mechanics, their interacting dynamics, and the player's aesthetic experience. Relevant passages: "thinking about the player encourages experience-driven (as opposed to feature-driven) design" and "components cannot be evaluated in vacuo, aside from their effects on a system behavior and player experience." Its tuning section calls for playtesting and iterative refinement of numerical values. It does not prove that a specific duration, modifier, threshold, or feature count is correct.
2. Ian Schreiber, [Level 12: Solo Testing](https://gamedesignconcepts.wordpress.com/2009/08/06/level-12-solo-testing/), HTTP 200. Separates bug, usability, balance, focus, and fun testing. Relevant passage: "A game can be usable, balanced and functional and still be uninteresting." It also warns that designers and familiar testers can miss obvious flaws. This is practitioner teaching, not a controlled empirical study, and some advice concerns tabletop games.
3. Ian Schreiber, [Level 15: Blindtesting](https://gamedesignconcepts.wordpress.com/2009/08/17/level-15-blindtesting/), HTTP 200. Defines testing without the designer present, warns of rule misunderstandings and feedback limitations, and recommends testers from the intended audience. Relevant passage: "Without you there, any issue the players run into is a show-stopper that prevents them from finishing." Its purpose here is to test discovery without helpful explanations, not to pretend that an agent reading source code is a blind player.

## What counts as a material decision

Every rule affecting a player's available actions, outcomes, learning, effort, recovery, interpretation, progression, or ownership belongs in the ledger. That includes hidden multipliers, caps, representation choices, defaults, reveal thresholds, simulation policy, and claims made about the result. Cosmetic code choices belong when they affect legibility, motion, contrast, or feedback. No one can responsibly claim that research empirically justifies every CSS pixel.

A decision may be retained as an authored hypothesis. That is a justification with a stated risk, not a finding that it is optimal. Each ledger entry needs:

- The exact rule and code location, including its constants or an explicitly listed family of constants.
- The desired player experience and causal argument connecting the rule to it.
- An adversarial objection or plausible alternative.
- The actual source principle, with its limits, separately from designer inference.
- Measured evidence, if any, and what it fails to measure.
- A disposition: retain with evidence, retain provisionally, revise, or remove. A fix remains unverified until its stated check passes.

Grouping is acceptable only if every member is named. "We balanced the economy" is insufficient for pricing, supply costs, wages, marketing, contracts, acquisition costs, and branch scaling. A blanket citation to MDA does not justify their coefficients.

## Priority findings

1. **P1, evidence boundary:** no current automated result proves "addicting", award quality, or equality with Universal Paperclips. No numerical coefficient can be declared optimal merely because a reviewer retrieved a design article. Final reporting must make this distinction clear. This is not a reason to stop implementation; it is a reason to describe what the work actually establishes.
2. **P1, novice economics:** the reported 18-worker run is a stronger adversarial scenario than six knowledgeable fresh players. Test it explicitly with nonoptimal price/purchases, discovery of procurement/marketing, and recovery when cash/forms reach zero. Its bottleneck crosses economy, progression, UX and automation ownership.
3. **P1, cross-era incentives:** continued retail cash can make the Network financial constraint and patent settlement nominal. A state can satisfy milestones while failing to create strategic tension. Retain the old economy only with an explicit purpose and sensitivity evidence.
4. **P2, coverage:** legacy progression, wage forgiveness, the absence of management over acquired staff, heuristic staffing order, rolling records, and aesthetic/accessibility details are easy to omit from domain ledgers. They are material decisions and must receive dispositions.
5. **P2, reward accounting:** letters, authorizations, actual throughput changes and ending progress are distinct. Calling every informational letter a reward can hide mechanical inactivity. Report longest intervals with and without new meaningful decisions.

## Verification boundary and next human test

This reviewer changed documentation only. No tests were removed, relaxed, or rewritten. Existing evidence was read rather than rerun, to avoid duplicating the owner's verification.

Before saying the game is fun, run independent target-audience sessions without source knowledge or designer coaching. Use separate QA saves. Record outcomes and reasons for stopping, not just completion times:

1. Fresh opening: locate filing, earn first automation, explain why money can arrive after a form is filed, and choose the next improvement. Ask what the player thinks will happen before the action.
2. The user's 18-worker state: explain the backlog, discover procurement and demand growth, recover a profitable supply loop without being told to lower prices. Record wrong turns, scrolling and navigation.
3. First genetics: predict which workers will change, choose a production and research profile, then evaluate the observed effect. Reject confusion between shared equipment and inheritance.
4. Late expansion: fill and replace a large cohort, preserve department staff, retain supply cash, and understand the current quote without hundreds of inputs.
5. Network: identify the initial growth bottleneck, deploy protocols, enable building, choose when to stop growth, and finish without source-informed thresholds or compulsory attendance.
6. Ending: explain what earlier promises changed. Ask which choice felt consequential and whether the player wants another run, rather than assuming the credits induce replay.

Mix players familiar and unfamiliar with incremental games. Do not lead with "this should be addictive" or award expectations. Follow the observed issues with fixes and another test. Agent review can challenge causal claims and find exploits; it cannot supply independent human feelings.
