# Automation, recovery, and recurring work

Independent adversarial review of procurement, rescue, filing cadence, orders, memos, staffing batches, reserves, and infrastructure delegation. Reviewed the implementation, ran deterministic counterexamples, and challenged the first corrections in a follow-up round. This is a design justification and challenge, not evidence of human enjoyment.

## Research actually retrieved

Retrieved on 2026-10-04:

1. Anthony Pecorella, [The Math of Idle Games, Part I](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-i), Game Developer, October 13, 2016. The article models income, costs, multipliers, and optimal purchases. It explicitly says optimal choices can be “far too micromanage-y for a human,” and that players “buy in bulk to simplify the decision process.” It also describes shifting the relative value of generators to produce variety. This supports batch actions and checking marginal returns. It does not prove a particular reserve, cooldown, or unlock price is good.
2. Frank Lantz's Universal Paperclips, [main.js](https://www.decisionproblem.com/paperclips/main.js) and [projects.js](https://www.decisionproblem.com/paperclips/projects.js). These are inspected primary implementation sources, not a controlled study. `WireBuyer` unlocks after 15 purchases for 7,000 operations and purchases wire when stock reaches one or less. `Beg for More Wire` grants a spool when liquid/portfolio funds cannot buy wire and wire/unsold clips are exhausted. It charges one trust and increases its own uses, permitting repeated relief. `AutoTourney` separately automates a later repeating activity. These examples support replacing mastered repetition and providing explicit insolvency recovery. They do not justify copying their exact thresholds or adopting a once-only rescue.

An attempted “Playing with efficiency” Game Developer URL returned a generic category page. It is not cited as research. The former Kongregate article URL returned 404; the Game Developer republication above was accessible.

## Reproduced objections

All counterexamples used real nonempty fixtures and public actions for setup. No tests were deleted or weakened. The deterministic random value 0.99 avoids breaks and gives automation a favorable case.

### Saturated retail automation spends money without earning more

Setup: `labFixture()`, three additional generalists (nine total), equipment level 12, price $5, 5,000 blank forms, $10,000 cash, automatic procurement enabled, contract renewal off. Advance 600 seconds with memos disabled/enabled.

| Result | Memos disabled | Memos enabled before correction |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash | $10,180.68 | $9,860.68 |
| Retail receipts | $1,506.68 | $1,506.68 |
| Purchased supplies | $840 | $1,080 |
| Inventory | 14,503.93 | 17,778.17 |

The automatic policy loses $320 with identical retail receipts. It creates 3,274 more unsold forms. This refutes the original assumption that recurring production boosts are harmless convenience.

Required correction: skip delegated memos with no marginal sales benefit and no chosen departmental/contract purpose. Retail forecasting must account for inventory already satisfying demand. If active contracts or department assignments are accepted as a purpose, explicitly distinguish useful research/influence/pressure relief from guaranteed positive cash returns. A nearly finished order or already-zero pressure is not evidence of marginal benefit.

Follow-up challenge to the first correction: the retail-return estimate assumes supplies for 90 seconds. In `labFixture()`, publicly transfer all but one headquarters employee, set cash $1,000 and blank forms one, disable procurement, enable automatic memos with orders off. The first guard estimates +$6.80 and circulates a $20 memo because some stock exists. Both boosted and unboosted production can sell the one available form, so the actual marginal revenue is zero. The delegated cash-benefit path must bound its estimate by available material or require the entire forecast window to be supplied.

### Emergency filing alone cannot restore a large payroll

Setup: `labFixture()`, expand capacity through public upgrade action, hire 34 additional generalists (40 total), then represent a spent company with cash, forms, and inventory zero; disable procurement and mark relief already claimed. Repeat public emergency filing followed by `advance(.35)` 40 times.

Result: 40 successful service requests, $0 accumulated cash, zero forms. The $3.60/s payroll consumes the maximum theoretical $3.57/s service receipts. Transferring employee 40 immediately yields $42, proving another legal recovery route exists. Thus this is not a permanent engine softlock, but “use emergency filing to rebuild your supply budget” is insufficient guidance for this real state. Calling the company recoverable must name the actual route.

Required correction: provide recovery that remains effective with payroll. For transferable workers, expose the necessary transfer route and its consequences in the stopped-production UI. Do not weaken the staffed case to an empty company when testing the remedy.

### Acquired payroll can create an actual permanent softlock

Setup: `conglomerateFixture()`, publicly acquire Memo Holdings after Forms & Sons and Desk Industries, publicly transfer all headquarters employees, then represent a spent company with cash/forms/inventory zero and supplier relief previously claimed. The resulting 84 acquired employees have $7.56/s payroll. No branch cohorts, active order, or Network phase provide another source of cash. Public actions cannot release the acquired legacy workforce.

Forty public emergency filings at the fastest theoretical cadence leave cash $0 and forms zero. Procurement cannot buy its $20 pack. Supplier relief rejects with instructions to use the emergency method that cannot work. There are no remaining headquarters or branch employees to transfer for funds. This is a genuine valid-state progression softlock, not merely misleading advice. An existing valid save can reach it after spending the last available funds and exhausting inventory.

Required correction: allow a bounded repeatable rescue, make recovery receipts survive payroll for supplies, or expose a reversible acquired-workforce transfer. Preserve meaningful operating costs and test this exact nonempty acquired-cohort state. The original one-time entitlement test does not cover this requirement.

### A safe throughput estimate can renew an unfunded order

Setup: `labFixture()`, capacity level 5, 250 additional generalists (256 total), equipment 12, price $5, zero forms/inventory, procurement enabled, national renewal enabled, memos disabled, $200,000 lifetime revenue, cash `operatingReserve + 1` ($732.20).

Expected output is 553.91/s. The old renewal guard starts the 30,000-form national order. After `advance(300)`: cash $0, forms zero, completed orders zero, failed orders one. Available funds cannot purchase the material and carry payroll until settlement. Merely enabling procurement does not make it funded.

Required correction: independently bound delivery funding from actual stocks, routed throughput, payroll, supply packs, and any conservative retail receipts. Test rejection of this case without changing the order size or excusing failure as unavoidable chance. A capacity margin still does not guarantee safety against future external shocks.

## Evidence limits

The source-supported principles are automation after learning, distinct production/exchange bottlenecks, reducing purchase micromanagement, and explicit recovery. Every exact unlock cost, buffer duration, cooldown, pack unit, and safety percentage above remains a local tuning hypothesis. Existing balance runs establish completion and interaction counts for informed scripted players; they do not establish that new players discover procurement quickly, like timing memos, or understand reserves. The original recovery and memo objections, plus the delivery-funding follow-up, have been independently rerun after correction. No unresolved correctness objection remains in these tested cases. Heuristic automation and human comprehension still require playtesting.


## Follow-up verification of corrections

- Saturated retail: delegation now refuses the harmful memo. Manual circulation remains available with explicit sales-return warning.
- One available form with procurement disabled: delegation now refuses the $20 memo despite the unconstrained retail-return estimate being positive.
- Acquired-workforce softlock: the original 84-person fixture now reaches $20 protected cash after 16 service requests, passes `GameSchema`, buys 250 forms, and clears its protected reserve. No employee traits or workforce were removed for the passing result.
- Protected reserve stores real earnings already included in cash, never a second currency or minted reimbursement. Payroll/upkeep cannot consume that balance while no production occurs. Protection ends on restocking/production and is capped by one pack cost. Old saves default the new field to zero. Players can deliberately spend the cash on something else; then further service requests still restore the pack budget.
- The original unfunded national order is rejected. Renewal independently requires reserve, missing supply packs, and estimated delivery payroll, without assuming hypothetical retail receipts.
- Targeted `bun test src/game/design-review.test.tsx src/game/experience.test.ts`: 35 passing tests, 322 assertions. This includes existing staffed, migration, reserve, and progression tests plus the new regression cases. No original tests were weakened.

### Follow-up correction: funding forecast omitted upkeep

The first delivery-funding correction omitted branch maintenance. Reproduced through public setup from `conglomerateFixture()`: capacity level five, 250 additional generalists (256 headquarters employees), eight empty branches upgraded to level eight, equipment level 12, price $1,000,000, zero forms/inventory, procurement and national renewal enabled, memos disabled. Empty offices are a legal player decision and still incur maintenance.

Conservative routed output is 709.47/s and estimated delivery duration 84.57 seconds. Branch upkeep is $32/s. Cash set to operating reserve plus missing supplies plus modeled payroll plus $1 ($7,851.89) passes the first funding guard. Advancing 300 seconds still produces one failed order, no completed orders, and zero cash.

Final disposition: corrected by including maintenance in the delivery operating-cost bound. Independently rerunning the exact $7,851.89 fixture now leaves the order idle. Targeted design-review tests pass after the correction. This resolves the direct omission in the promised forecast; future shocks still legitimately remain outside the guarantee.
